ABSTRACT This paper proposes a tractable and compact generative model for cetacean whistle signals
based on Variational Autoencoder (VAE) and mixture of Gaussians in underwater biomimetic communica-
tion. Cetacean whistles are nonlinear frequency-modulated signals with diverse time-frequency structures
and can be interpreted as probability density functions. To learn the marginal probability distribution of
whistle data, this study incorporates a VAE and a mixture of Gaussians. The VAE learns latent space
representations from whistle spectrogram images, and the prior distribution of the latent variables is
modeled using multiple Gaussian components. Since the latent space of whistle contour is modeled as
an explicit density function of multimodal structure, it enables unsupervised clustering and conditional
whistle generation by analyzing component-wise features. The statistical distance between the synthesized
whistles and the training data was evaluated using the Fréchet inception distance, Recall, Precision, and F1-
score yielding a score of 2.6767, 87.21%, 91.99%, and 89.54%, respectively, when the number of Gaussian
components was set to 10,000.
INDEX TERMS Cetacean whistle, conditional generative model, generative model, mixture of Gaussians,
variational autoencoder, biomimetic underwater communication.
I. INTRODUCTION
A. BRIEF INTRODUCTION OF BIOMIMETIC UNDERWATER
COMMUNICATION
Underwater biomimetic communication is a technique that
emulates acoustic signals produced by marine mammals,
primarily utilizing cetacean clicks and whistles [1], [2],
[3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9]. Cetacean whistles are non-
linear frequency-modulated signals characterized by time-
varying frequencies, which appear as diverse contours in
spectrograms. Previous studies have proposed communi-
cation schemes that replicate the structural patterns of
whistle contours or have adapted conventional digital mod-
ulation techniques for biomimetic purposes [1], [2], [3],
[4], [5], [6], [7]. In contrast, clicks are broadband pulse

signals, and to fully exploit their wideband characteristics,
time-delay modulation—where information bits are encoded
in the time intervals between pulses—has been predomi-

However, click sounds are generally unsuitable for
biomimetic communication due to their broadband charac-
teristics [37]. This limitation arises for two primary reasons.
First, click signals have a relatively short propagation range.
In particular, their high-frequency components experience
significant attenuation due to underwater transmission loss,
making them unsuitable for long-range communication over
several kilometers. In contrast, whistles—with their relatively
narrowband nature—are more favorable for long-distance
transmission. Second, there are design constraints in under-
water acoustic projectors. Most current projectors have
difficulty generating very short-duration broadband pulses
effectively.

Biomimetic communication enables underwater platforms
to exchange information covertly. Communication signals
that mimic the characteristics and structure of biological
sounds may be detected or intercepted by non-cooperative
platforms; however, they are misidentified as biological
emissions and subsequently ignored. Therefore, the precise
modeling of the probability density function of biological
signals is essential to ensure the covert nature of biomimetic
communication. In this context, it is important to first collect
a sufficient number of biological signal samples x, estimate
their marginal probability distribution p (x), and then gener-
ate synthetic communication signals that follow the estimated
distribution.
B. MOTIVATION OF THIS WORK
Whistle-based
biomimetic
communication
requires
the
acquisition of a large dataset of whistle contours from the
target species to preserve covertness. The communication
signal can then be constructed by either selecting a finite set
of representative whistles for modulation or by employing
generative methods that replicate the underlying probability
distribution of real whistles. Nevertheless, some biomimetic
communication approaches are inherently limited in their
ability to faithfully mimic real whistle contours. For example,
the modulation scheme called Binary Orthogonal Keying
(BOK), proposed in [10], divides a whistle signal into fixed
symbol intervals and modulates each symbol using two types
of chirp signals, each centered around the whistle frequency
of the corresponding interval. However, such approaches that
modify the original whistle signal may reduce its similar-
ity to real whistle signals, posing a limitation in terms of
biomimicry.
In contrast, there have been efforts to utilize real whis-
tle contours directly. Ahn et al. [11] extracted and selected
whistle signals from white-sided dolphins and applied them
to a machine learning-based biomimetic communication
framework. In their study, the whistles were grouped, and
communication symbols were assigned based on the transi-
tions between these groups. A total of 704 selected whistles
were used in the system.
However, repeating a limited set of whistle patterns using
underwater acoustic projectors can compromise the covert
nature of biomimetic communication. Deterministic meth-
ods that select and transmit from a fixed set of signals
may allow adversarial systems—designed to continuously
monitor and analyze biological acoustic emissions—to iden-
tify recurring patterns. Such deterministic signal generation
approaches increase the risk of exposing not only the pres-
ence of biomimetic communication but also its modulation
schemes and system parameters. The advancement of deep
learning-based artificial intelligence technologies further
amplifies this risk.
One approach to mitigating the limitations of using fixed
whistle patterns, as mentioned earlier, is to model the whistle
shapes as a probability density function and to utilize newly
generated data sampled from this distribution. Although the
sampled whistles resemble the original patterns, they are not
exact replicas, making them more likely to be perceived as
natural biological signals.
Accordingly, the objective of this study is to design a deep
generative model for biomimetic underwater communication
that can be deployed on surface or underwater platforms
and that faithfully replicates the whistle sounds of observed
whales or dolphins. Specifically, the model is designed to
follow the estimated probability distribution of the collected
whistle data. By leveraging the proposed approach, there is
a higher chance that non-cooperative platforms will inter-
pret the generated signals as genuine biological emissions,
thereby decreasing the risk of the transmitting platform being
exposed.
C. RELATED WORK
Considering whistle data in the image domain, such as spec-
trograms, the whistle generation technique addressed in this
study can be implemented using widely adopted deep genera-
tive models [12], [13], [14], [15], [16]. Currently, commonly
used models for image generation include energy-based mod-
els [12], variational autoencoders (VAEs) [13], normalizing
flows [14], generative adversarial networks (GANs) [15], and
diffusion models [16], [17].
To the best of our knowledge, GANs are the only approach
of deep learning-based generative model that has been
applied to cetacean whistle generation [18], [19], [20]. These
GAN-based methods were introduced to avoid repeated use
of identical whistle sounds or to mitigate the issue of limited
training data [18], [19], [20]. Zhang et al. [18] trained a Deep
WaveGAN to generate waveform-level bottlenose dolphin
whistles and evaluated the similarity between synthetic and
real data using various time- and frequency-domain metrics.
Wang et al. [19] addressed the data scarcity problem by
applying various augmentation techniques to a small set of
bottlenose dolphin whistles and trained a masking autoen-
coder and GAN to generate high-quality signals. Kim et al.
[20] proposed WhistleGAN, which combines Mel filter banks
with a deep convolutional GAN, to avoid the repetition of
whistle sounds in biomimetic communication and presented
a computer simulation for biomimetic communication based
on synthetic biological signals. All three studies [18], [19],
[20], which rely on the inherent characteristics of GANs,
enable the generation of high-quality whistle data at relatively
high speed.
However, GANs suffer from two fundamental limitations.
The first is mode collapse. Because GANs generate data
through a minimax game between the generator and the
discriminator, the generator may learn to produce only a
narrow subset of data that can fool the discriminator [21].
Mode collapse is a well-known issue intrinsic to GANs, and
thus incorporating them into the transmission architecture
of biomimetic communication introduces practical con-
straints. Second, GANs do not explicitly model probability
distributions. As a result, even after training a whistle gener-
ation model, the underlying distribution remains intractable.

In contrast, explicit modeling of probability distributions
enables additional capabilities such as density estimation,
likelihood calculation, and unsupervised clustering—features
that GANs do not inherently support.
The VAE and Mixture of Gaussians-based approach pro-
posed in this study has not been utilized in the bio-signal
generation field but has primarily been considered within
the machine learning community [22], [23], [24], [25],
[26], [27], [28], [38], [39]. Specifically, modeling the prior
distribution p (z) of the latent variables in a VAE using a mul-
timodal structure such as a mixture of Gaussians enhances
the expressiveness of the generative model for complex
data, enables unsupervised clustering. Dilokthanakul et al.
[22] proposed the Gaussian Mixture Variational Autoencoder
(GMVAE), which models the latent prior as a Gaussian
Mixture Model (GMM) and introduces both discrete and
continuous higher-level latent variables, enabling the model
to capture natural and interpretable cluster structures in an
unsupervised setting. They also analyzed the issue of cluster
degeneracy caused by over-regularization in standard VAEs
and introduced a simple heuristic to effectively mitigate
this problem. Jiang et al. [23] proposed Variational Deep
Embedding (VaDE), an unsupervised generative clustering
framework that incorporates a GMM prior into VAE archi-
tecture, enabling joint learning of latent representations and
cluster assignments while retaining the model’s generative
capacity.
However, the approaches presented in [22], [23], [24], [25],
[26], [27], and [28] require a rapidly increasing number of
learnable parameters as the number of clusters grows. For the
large-scale clustering level (approximately 10,000 clusters)
considered in this study, such methods become computa-
tionally inefficient. For example, both GMVAE and VaDE
simultaneously learn a mean vector and a covariance matrix
for each cluster during network training, resulting in a sub-
stantial increase in learnable parameters as the number of
clusters rises. Similarly, VampPrior must learn a pseudo-input
corresponding to each cluster, with the same dimensionality
as the input image, which further increases the parameter
count. Although these existing methods may effectively cap-
ture the latent space when using a relatively small number
of clusters, employing a larger number of clusters is more
advantageous for achieving fine-grained and flexible condi-
tional generation.
D. CONTRIBUTIONS OF THE STUDY
This paper proposes a VAE-GMM framework for modeling
the marginal probability distribution of whistle image data
from false killer whales (Pseudorca crassidens). The whistle
data generated by the proposed VAE-GMM exhibit a high
degree of similarity to real biological signals. When evalu-
ated using the Fréchet Inception Distance (FID), Recall, and
Precision metrics, the proposed model outperformed existing
methods while maintaining a comparable generation speed.
Unlike traditional biomimetic communication approaches
that repeatedly transmit a limited set of playback signals,
the proposed VAE-GMM based whistle generation method
enables a more authentic biomimetic reproduction of real
biological sounds.
Furthermore, because the VAE-GMM organizes data
according to the morphological characteristics of whistles,
it inherently provides the effect of unsupervised clustering.
During training, the VAE maps whistles with similar struc-
tural patterns to neighboring regions in the latent space. When
this latent space is subsequently modeled using a GMM, sim-
ilar patterns are grouped into the same Gaussian component.
Moreover, the proposed VAE-GMM supports highly flex-
ible conditional whistle generation. Since each Gaussian
component—or a group of components—can represent a spe-
cific whistle shape, generating desired whistle types becomes
feasible by sampling from specific components. Conditional
generation becomes feasible by analyzing the whistle char-
acteristics such as whistle length, minimum and maximum
frequency, start and end frequency, bandwidth, and cen-
ter frequency. By analyzing other whistle characteristics in
advance, the model can be easily extended to generate new
samples under specific conditions.
The conditional generation mechanism of the VAE-GMM
offers two key advantages over existing approaches such
as Conditional GANs (CGANs) and Conditional Diffusion
Models (CDMs) that employ classifier guidance or classifier-
free guidance [40], [41]. First, VAE-GMM does not require
explicit class label assignments for conditional generation.
By modeling the latent space with a GMM, the images are
automatically clustered and categorized. In contrast, CGANs
and CDMs must be trained with predefined class labels as
conditioning inputs. Since whistle patterns exhibit highly
diverse and continuous variations, it is practically infeasi-
ble to predefine discrete classes for them, which further
highlights the advantage of VAE-GMM in eliminating the
need for class assignment. Second, VAE-GMM demonstrates
superior flexibility in conditional generation. In CGANs and
CDMs, the predefined labels serve as the sole mechanism
for controlling conditional synthesis, which inherently limits
their ability to generalize to diverse generation conditions.
In contrast, the proposed method can flexibly generate data
under various conditions by analyzing the characteristics of
each Gaussian component.
The proposed method is clearly distinguished from existing
cetacean whistle generation models [18], [19], [20] in that
it enables flexible and scalable conditional generation. Since
the models in [18], [19], and [20] are based on GANs, their
conditional generation mechanism is limited to CGANs, and
their underlying probability distributions remain intractable.
In contrast, the relatively simple combination of VAE and
GMM offers greater flexibility and efficiency in conditional
generation, primarily due to the characteristics of the whistle
image dataset. Unlike large-scale vision datasets such as
ImageNet or COCO, the whistle image dataset contains less
information. Although the whistle contours are diverse in

B. DATA REPRESENTATION
The domain of the target whistle data x can take various
forms. In this study, we adopt a representation in which each
whistle is expressed as a single image depicting the contour of
a baseband whistle signal with the center frequency removed
as in Fig. 1.
The time-frequency contour information embedded in
the image-based whistle data can be easily converted into
a time-frequency vector using simple thresholding tech-
niques. Based on this vector, the final nonlinear frequency-
modulated (NFM) signal can be synthesized. Fig. 2 illustrates
the process of extracting a time-frequency vector from a
single-whistle image using thresholding, and generating a
constant-amplitude NFM signal based on this vector.
C. WHISTLE DATASET
To construct a whistle spectrogram dataset for the false
killer whale, this study utilized the Detection, Classification,
Localization and Density Estimation (DCLDE) 2022 Raw
Passive Acoustic Data, which was publicly released online
by the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration
(NOAA) [29]. The dataset comprises underwater acoustic
recordings collected using a multi-channel towed hydrophone
array during the Hawaiian Islands Cetacean and Ecosystem
Assessment Survey (HICEAS) conducted between July and
November 2017. The recordings were obtained alongside
visual line-transect surveys aimed at estimating cetacean
abundance and density.
For this study, we selected 2,486 acoustic files containing
whistles from the false killer whale as the target data. Each
file generally contains 60 seconds of underwater acoustic
signals measured from six hydrophones arranged in a towed
array configuration, and the sampling rate is 500 kHz.
Fig. 3 illustrates the overall process of constructing the
whistle dataset from raw acoustic signals. This process con-
sists of three main stages: spectrogram conversion, whistle
extraction, and dataset preparation.
In the first stage, the raw acoustic signals contain-
ing whistles are converted into spectrograms, allowing the
time-frequency characteristics of the whistles to be visually
identified. The spectrogram was generated after resampling
the signal to 192 kHz, applying a 2,048-point Hamming
window, and using a 50% overlap. As a result, the time
and frequency resolutions were approximately 5.3 ms and
93.75 Hz, respectively. To suppress low-frequency ocean
noise, a high-pass filter with a passband above 2 kHz was
applied.
In the second stage, a neural network-based whistle extrac-
tion method [31] was applied to detect individual whistle
contours. The extraction process was combined with a
post-processing algorithm [32], [33] that converts detection
outputs into individual whistle tracks.
In the final stage, the extracted whistle time-frequency
vectors were converted into individual whistle images to
construct the final training dataset. A total of 228,673 whis-
tle time-frequency vectors were obtained from 2,486 raw
acoustic signal files. Each vector was then used to gen-
erate a constant-amplitude NFM baseband signal and its
spectrogram, which was further transformed into a 128 ×
128 grayscale image (See Figs. 1 and 3).
D. LEARNING VARIATIONAL AUTOENCODER
VAEs are probabilistic generative models that learn a latent
variable representation of data. The VAE consists of an
encoder network that approximates the intractable true
posterior distribution p (z | x) with a tractable variational dis-
tribution q (z | x), and a decoder that models the conditional
likelihood p (x | z). The model is trained by maximizing the
Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO), which jointly encourages
accurate reconstruction of the data and regularization of the
latent space via the KL divergence between the approximate
posterior and the prior p (z).
To control the strength of latent regularization, a weighting
factor β can be applied to the regularization term LKL in the
VAE loss. The total loss function L (x) is then given by the
following expression:

as it explicitly models the prior distribution of latent variables.

2) VAE-GMM WHISTLE GENERATIVE MODEL
To quantitatively assess the generation quality of the proposed
VAE-GMM and its similarity to the real whistle dataset,
we adopted the FID, Recall, Precision, and F1-score. FID
is a widely used statistical metric for evaluating the fidelity
and diversity of synthesized images [36]. It computes the
distance between real and generated image distributions by
comparing the 2,048-dimensional activation vectors from the
final pooling layer of a pre-trained Inception V3 network.
Lower FID values indicate higher similarity between the
real and generated data. Recall, Precision, and F1-score are
quantified following the method proposed in [47].
We computed FID using two approaches. The first method,
denoted as FID1, binarized both the real and generated images
using a threshold of 0.1 before inputting them into the Incep-
tion V3 network. This approach aims to mitigate performance
distortion caused by background noise or irrelevant visual
details unrelated to the generation of whistle contour. The sec-
ond method, FID2, fed the original grayscale images directly
into the network without any thresholding, which is the more
standard practice in FID evaluation. However, because the
shape and diversity of whistle contours are the primary focus
in our study, we considered FID1 to be a more meaningful
evaluation metric.
Since the Inception V3 model expects RGB input images of
size (224,224,3), we resized the original 128×128 grayscale
images using bicubic interpolation and replicated the single
channel to create a three-channel RGB input.
Table 2 reports the FID scores, Recall, Precision, F1-score
for the VAE-GMM when the number of GMM components
was set to 1,000, 5,000, 10,000 and 15,000. The results
show that increasing the number of components consistently
improved the metrics. All performance metrics showed the
most improved results when K = 15,000.
Table 2 also summarizes the performance analysis results
based on the β weight of the KL divergence term in the loss
function. Focusing on FID1 and F1-score, the performance
was overall superior when β
=10−4 compared to when
β =10−2, and β =10−6.
It is also notable that there was a substantial difference
between FID1 and FID2, which implies that fine image details
significantly influence FID. Given that the main objective
of this work is to generate accurate time-frequency contours
rather than detailed pixel-level images, we argue that FID1—
excluding background and irrelevant variations—is a more
appropriate performance indicator for our application.

V. DISCUSSIONS
The proposed VAE-GMM-based whistle generation method
has two major limitations, as described below:
First, whistle signal generation currently requires a
four-step conversion process—from whistle image genera-
tion to waveform synthesis. Since our ultimate goal is to
generate whistle waveforms that can be directly transmitted
through an underwater acoustic projector, a more efficient
future approach would be to train a model capable of gen-
erating the transmit waveform directly.
Second, the proposed VAE-GMM learns only the contour
shape of whistles with their center frequencies removed.
To transmit signals through an actual underwater acoustic
projector, an additional method for assigning center fre-
quencies is required. We attempted to train the model using
spectrogram images that include the center frequency; how-
ever, this approach was found to be ineffective.
Specifically,
when
the
center
frequency
was
not
removed—meaning the whistle contour could shift ver-
tically within the image—the data variability increased
substantially. As a result, the VAE reconstruction loss was
sufficiently reduced only when the latent dimension was
set to much higher values (e.g., 128 or 256) compared to
the proposed 64-dimensional configuration. However, when
the latent dimension exceeded 128, we found that model-
ing the latent space with much more than 10,000 Gaussian
components became computationally impractical due to the
computational complexity of the EM algorithm.
Therefore, we expect that assigning the center frequency of
the data contributing to each Gaussian component and sam-
pling the center frequency from this distribution would serve
as an effective alternative. We leave a detailed investigation
of this issue for future work.
VI. CONCLUSION
This paper proposed a VAE-GMM-based method for gener-
ating whistle image data of false killer whale. The proposed
approach explicitly models the latent space of whistle images
as a mixture of Gaussians, enabling conditional generation
through analysis of component-wise features, such as min-
imum and maximum frequency, start and end frequency,
bandwidth, center frequency, and duration. It was shown that
the conditional probability distribution can be approximately
estimated based on the characteristics of the selected Gaus-
sian components. Furthermore, the analytical tractability of
the mixture density enables the use of MAP estimation for
potential applications in unsupervised clustering and classifi-
cation.
The proposed VAE-GMM supports fast whistle data gener-
ation, comparable to GAN-based methods, while offering an
intuitive mechanism to control the generation process through
the mixture components. The method demonstrated com-
petitive performance, suggesting its effectiveness for future
applications in biomimetic underwater communication sys-
tems.
We anticipate that the proposed VAE-GMM-based whistle
generation method will serve as an effective tool for the
linguistic analysis and understanding of cetacean acoustic
signals. A systematic method for structuring these signals
is essential for their linguistic interpretation. While conven-
tional studies have predominantly relied on manual pattern
analysis through direct observation, our approach offers a
novel framework for structuring whistle morphologies in
an unsupervised manner. We believe that this method will
provide a foundational basis for future research, such as iden-
tifying correlations between distinct whistles and establishing
links between acoustic signals and their behavioral contexts.

B. TRAINING CONFIGURATION AND NETWORK

The training of VAE-GMM was conducted using MATLAB
R2024a, and computations were performed in parallel across
seven NVIDIA V100 GPUs.
For VAE training, the learning rate was set to 10−3, and
the β coefficient was set to 10−4. The mini-batch size was
128 per GPU, resulting in a total of 896 samples per batch.
The WGAN-GP weight λ was set to 1, and the critic was
trained three times per iteration of the VAE. The total number
of training epochs was 300. The gradient penalty coefficient
λGP was set to 5. The learning rate for the critic was 2×10−4,
with a gradient decay factor of 0 and a squared gradient decay
factor of 0.9. The Adam optimizer was employed for training
both the VAE and the critic network.
During the training process, a data augmentation technique
was applied in which each image was randomly rotated by
0◦, 90◦, 180◦, or 270◦with equal probability. This approach
was adopted to prevent the VAE from overly focusing on
horizontal line features, which can be prevalent in whistle
image data, and to encourage balanced learning of diverse
directional features, such as vertical lines.
APPENDIX B
PARAMETER ESTIMATION OF GAUSSIAN MIXTURE
MODEL
The parameters of the Gaussian mixture model consist of the
mean vectors, covariance matrices, and mixing coefficients
corresponding to the specified Gaussian components. These
parameters are estimated using the EM algorithm. In this
study, the maximum number of EM iterations was set to
1,500, and the probability tolerance for early stopping was
set to 10−8.
